The Mystery of Wormwood

Wormwood, Artemisia absinthium, is a silvery, bitter herb of the daisy family. It is native to Europe, and it has been woven into cup, cloth and ritual for a very long time.

A name with a story

The English name comes from the Old English wermod, a word tied to the herb’s old use against intestinal worms. The bitterness is part of its character and its lore: in the Book of Revelation, a star called Wormwood falls from heaven and turns the waters bitter.

In the cup

Wormwood is the signature ingredient of absinthe and a classic flavouring of vermouth, and it has long been used as a bittering herb in brewing. Its sharp, clean note is why it works in drinks that are meant to wake the palate.

In ritual

Across many cultures, aromatic herbs of the Artemisia family have been hung, bundled and burned to clear a space and mark a threshold. In East Asia the related mugwort (艾, ai) is hung over doors at the Dragon Boat Festival and burned as incense, to turn away ill luck.

On charcoal

Burned as a powder, wormwood gives a green, herbal, slightly bitter smoke. It is a good counterpoint to the warmth of sandalwood and the depth of agarwood, and it suits moments when you want a room to feel clear rather than soft.
